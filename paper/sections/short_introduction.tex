\section{Introduction}
Task-specific vision models offer efficient predictions, while general vision--language models (VLMs) can handle a broader range of inputs at greater computational cost. Their strengths need not coincide: a specialist benefits from task-specific training, whereas a VLM may resolve examples that require broader visual or semantic knowledge. This raises a practical question: when is a VLM call worth its cost? Calling it selectively may improve accuracy without paying for every input, a motivation also reflected in prompt-routing services~\cite{bedrockrouting}.

We study this question for visual tasks in which a specialist first predicts a class, a set of boxes or an event label. A router decides whether to keep that answer or replace it with the VLM's answer. Low specialist confidence is a natural reason to seek another opinion, but it does not tell us whether that opinion will be better. Both models may fail on the same example, and replacement can overturn an answer that was already correct. Conversely, even a VLM with lower average accuracy can help on examples that match its strengths. The value of collaboration therefore depends on which errors the models share and which they can correct for each other.

A limited call budget makes this an ordering problem: the router must rank inputs by the value of replacing their answers. As the budget grows, the chosen set expands beyond the most promising inputs to examples with smaller or uncertain gains. A useful evaluation should therefore examine the full performance--call curve. It also matters how a trained router turns its outputs into a ranking, as training supervision alone does not determine that ordering.

Prior work provides several starting points. Confidence estimation~\cite{calibration} and selective prediction~\cite{selectivenet} identify uncertain inputs, while learning to defer accounts for an imperfect expert~\cite{defer,calibrateddefer}. Its joint-classification formulations learn class outputs alongside deferral and need adaptation for box or event outputs. Post-hoc deferral~\cite{posthocdefer} keeps an existing predictor fixed, making it a close comparison for answer replacement. We adapt its loss to our router inputs and backbone. Text and multimodal routing~\cite{frugal,routellm,cascaderouting,vlrouterbench,mmrbench,relope,streamsense} study related choices across other model interfaces and tasks. Speculative decoding~\cite{specdecode,specsampling} instead verifies draft tokens within generation.

We introduce \textbf{Learned Routing (LR)}, a compact router using input features and the specialist's representations, predictions and confidence. Paired answers show when replacement helps or hurts. Inference uses information available before the VLM call. We compare training objectives and ranking scores separately, and use a normalized estimate of replacement gain as the default score. Our contributions are:

\noindent\textbf{(1) Unified evaluation.} Across four tasks and 16 model pairs, we explore different training objectives and ranking scores within the LR framework, identifying where collaboration helps and where it offers little benefit.

\noindent\textbf{(2) Method and finding.} We develop LR with normalized scoring and show that, on some tasks, score construction affects performance more than the tested training objectives.

\noindent\textbf{(3) Open-source release.} We release all experiment code and per-example data, the 80 main-table router checkpoints, and reproduction materials.
